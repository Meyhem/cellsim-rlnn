Reinforcement learning, from zero

How CellSim learns to eat

A tiny blob with a tiny brain starts out swimming at random in a petri dish. Half a thousand rounds of practice later it hunts food on purpose. This page walks through every moving part, with no maths background assumed.

The whole idea in one loop

CellSim uses reinforcement learning. Nobody tells the cell how to find food. We only give it points when it eats, and let it try things. Behaviour that earns points becomes more likely next time.

1. The dish

The world is a circle of radius 1. Twenty food pellets sit inside it. Whenever the cell touches one, the pellet is eaten and a new one appears somewhere else, so the dish never runs out. Each episode lasts 500 steps and the score is the number of pellets eaten.

Movement is slippery on purpose. The cell has momentum and drag (drag = 0.85), so it cannot stop or turn instantly. Its top speed is accel / (1 − drag) ≈ 0.067 per step, which means crossing the dish takes about 30 steps. That is why it has to plan a little instead of just twitching toward food.

2. What the cell sees

The cell has no map and no camera. It shoots 16 invisible rays, evenly spaced around its body, each reaching a quarter of the way across the dish (0.5 units). For every ray it senses two things: how close is the nearest food, and how close is the wall. Closer means a bigger number, from 0 (nothing in reach) up to 1 (touching).

Five more numbers describe its own body: speed forward, speed sideways, what it did last step, and how much of the episode is left. Together that is 16 + 16 + 5 = 37 numbers per step. This list is called the observation.

3. The brain

The brain is a small neural network. Think of it as a function with thousands of dials. Numbers go in one end, pass through layers that mix and squash them, and two numbers come out. Training means turning the dials until the outputs lead to good behaviour.

The actor

Outputs the average turn and thrust it would like. It does not output a single fixed action. It outputs a bell curve of possible actions and the cell draws a random sample from it. That randomness is how it tries new things.

The critic

Never moves the cell. It only predicts "from this situation, how much food will I probably eat in the near future?" The actor will use this prediction as a yardstick for whether an outcome was a pleasant surprise or a disappointment.

tanh is a squashing function that keeps every internal number between −1 and 1. Without a squashing step between layers, stacking layers would add no power.

4. Exploration: the spread of the dice

The actor's randomness has a size, called σ (sigma). Early on it is large, so the cell flails in every direction and stumbles on food by luck. As the cell learns, σ shrinks and its behaviour becomes decisive. This is measured by entropy, a score for how random a policy is. The curves below are the real values from the training run.

Most steps give nothing at all, and food only shows up after a sequence of correct turns. Working out which earlier step deserves credit for a later reward is the central difficulty of reinforcement learning, and it is the reason for the next two ideas.

6. Looking ahead: value and discounting

A pellet eaten right now is worth more than one eaten 100 steps from now, because the far future is uncertain. The discount γ = 0.99 multiplies each step of delay by 0.99, so the weight on a reward k steps away is 0.99k.

The critic learns to predict this discounted total. Then for any action we can ask: did it work out better or worse than the critic expected? That difference is called the advantage. Positive means "do more of that". Negative means "do less".

7. One epoch of training

Learning repeats the same cycle 500 times. Each pass is one epoch, and the file train.py runs it:

Step A produces 128 × 1,024 = 131,072 moves, each stored with the situation, the action taken, and the reward. Step B labels every move with its advantage. Step C uses those labelled moves to adjust the brain, and sweeps through the data four times in 16 chunks of 8,192 moves (64 small adjustments).

Why 1,024 dishes at once? The simulator stores every dish as one big block of numbers, so a GPU updates all of them in a single operation. That is what lets training run at hundreds of thousands of steps per second.

Inside one epoch, step by step

Here is a single round in slow motion, using epoch 100 of the real run as the example. The whole thing takes about a quarter of a second on the GPU.

A. Play: fill a table of experience

All 1,024 dishes advance together for 128 steps. At every step, for every dish, the code writes one row of notes. Nothing is learned yet. The brain is frozen and only plays.

The logprob is a snapshot of how likely the old brain thought that action was. We keep it so that step C can later measure how much the brain has changed (the PPO ratio). After 128 steps the code also asks the critic for one more value, for the state after the last step. Step B needs it.

B. Score: give every move an advantage

For each dish, the code walks backwards through its 128 steps. At each step it asks two questions. First, how surprised was the critic? That is the delta: reward received + discounted guess for the next state − the guess for this state. Second, what about the surprises that came after? Those get blended in, fading by γ·λ per step. A toy example with 5 steps and a pellet eaten at step 4:

The pellet at step 4 produced a big surprise (+0.50), and that surprise is passed back to the earlier steps, shrinking a little each step. Steps 1 to 3 were all part of the approach, so they get credit too. This is how a reward that arrives late still teaches the moves that led to it. The toy example uses raw rewards. In the real code rewards are first divided by their typical size (RewardScaler).

The step also builds returns, which equal advantage + the critic's guess. They are the target the critic is trained toward in step C.

C. Adjust: 64 small nudges

The 131,072 labelled moves are flattened into one long list, shuffled, and cut into 16 chunks of 8,192. Each chunk gives one nudge. The whole list is swept 4 times, so 16 × 4 = 64 nudges per epoch.

The loss is a single number saying how wrong the brain currently is, and backpropagation works out, for each of the 13,400 dials, which direction would lower that number. Then the optimiser takes a small step that way. Averaged over the epoch, the code reports these health numbers. They are a good way to check that learning is going smoothly:

D. Housekeeping

  • The input normaliser takes in this epoch's observations, so next epoch's inputs are scaled a bit better.
  • The learning rate shrinks a little: lr × (1 − epoch / 500). Big steps early, careful steps late.
  • A metrics line is appended to metrics.jsonl (score, losses, speed). That is what the charts above plot.
  • Every 10th epoch, the brain plays one extra deterministic episode on the fixed dish (no dice), and a replay and checkpoint are saved. The viewer's slider is built from these.

The one thing to remember: each epoch the brain plays first with fixed dice, then looks back at what happened, and then makes a few small corrections. Over 500 such rounds the corrections add up.

8. PPO: improve without breaking things

Proximal Policy Optimisation is the rule used in step C. The risk with learning from a pile of experience is overreaction: one lucky episode could push the brain far in a bad direction and ruin what it already knows.

PPO measures the ratio between how likely the new brain is to take an action versus how likely the old brain was. If the ratio goes beyond 0.8 to 1.2 (clip_eps = 0.2), the reward for pushing further is cut off. The brain can improve, but only in small, safe steps.

The total loss that gets minimised combines two jobs: make good actions more likely (the actor), and make the critic's predictions match what really happened (value_loss). Both are applied by the Adam optimiser with learning rate 0.0003, which shrinks linearly to zero over the run.

9. What actually happened

These charts come from a real run saved in this folder: 500 epochs, about 65 million simulated steps.

How to read a plateau. The score stops rising even though entropy keeps falling. The brain is still getting more decisive, but the dish itself limits the score: with this much momentum, a cell can only turn and reach food so fast. In practice this is where you would try a larger brain, a longer ray, or a changed dish rather than just more epochs.

10. Small tricks that keep training stable

Neural networks are fussy about the size of the numbers they receive. The code includes several routine fixes:

TrickWhat it does
RunningNormKeeps a running average and spread of the 37 inputs and rescales them, so no input drowns out the others.
RewardScalerRescales rewards by their typical size, so the same settings work whatever the scoring scale is.
Advantage normalisingWithin each chunk, advantages are rescaled to average 0, so roughly half the moves are pushed up and half down.
Gradient clippingCaps the size of any single adjustment (max_grad_norm = 0.5).
Orthogonal initStarts the dials in a well-behaved arrangement, with the actor's output tiny so early actions are near neutral.
Random start timesThe first batch starts dishes at random points in the episode, so training data is not all "beginning of episode".
